# The padel glossary.

The scoring terms, Spanish shot names, wall language, tactical shorthand and social-format phrases people tend to use as if everybody already knows them.

Definitions are written for players, not copied from a rulebook. Official rule terms are checked against current FIP guidance.

## Scoring and match terms

### Advantage

The point after deuce in traditional scoring. Win the next point and you win the game; lose it and the score returns to deuce.

### Break

Winning a game while the opponents are serving.

### Break point

A point where the receiving pair can win the opponents' service game.

### Deuce

40-40. What happens next depends on the scoring format being used.

### Game

A unit within a set. Traditional point scoring progresses love, 15, 30 and 40.

### Golden Point

A deciding-point format used instead of continued advantage play. Exact competition rules should be checked rather than assumed.

### Love

Zero in traditional scoring.

### Match point

A point that would end the match if won by the leading pair.

### Set

A group of games, normally won at six games with a two-game lead or via a tie-break at 6-6.

### Set point

A point that would complete the set if won.

### Star Point

The FIP scoring system introduced across specified circuits in 2026. It keeps limited advantage play before a deciding point at the third deuce.

### Super tie-break

A longer tie-break, commonly to ten points by two, sometimes agreed as a deciding-set replacement.

### Tie-break

A numerical point game, normally first to seven by two, commonly used at 6-6.

## Serving and rally terms

### First serve

The server's first attempt to begin a point legally.

### Second serve

The additional serve allowed after a first-service fault.

### Fault

An illegal serve or action that causes the point to be lost or, on first serve, produces a second serve.

### Let

A situation where the serve or point is replayed. The common example is a net-cord serve that otherwise lands legally.

### Return

The receiver's first shot after the serve. The serve must bounce before it is returned.

### Return position

The receiver's starting area and stance before the serve. It is normally deep enough to handle a serve near the back glass while still allowing a controlled first contact.

### Server

The player beginning the point. The same player serves throughout a game before service rotates according to the agreed order.

### Volley

Playing the ball before it bounces. Legal during a rally, but not for the return of serve.

## Court and wall terms

### Back glass

The rear glass wall. After the ball bounces on the court, a rebound from it can remain live.

### Double glass

A rally ball that bounces in court and then uses two wall surfaces, normally the back and side glass, before being returned.

### Corner glass

The back-and-side-wall area where one- or two-wall rebounds can change the ball's direction sharply.

### Fence / mesh

The metal enclosure around parts of the court. Rally rebounds can be legal after a floor bounce; serving rules are more restrictive.

### Net position

The attacking area close enough to volley effectively while still allowing reaction to a lob.

### No-man's-land

The awkward middle area between defence and net attack where balls frequently arrive at a player's feet.

### Out-of-court play

Retrieving a legal ball after it leaves the enclosure through an authorised opening. Only possible where the court and safety area allow it.

### Service box

The diagonal target area where a legal serve must first bounce.

### Service line

The line behind which the server begins and which defines the rear boundary of the service boxes.

### Transition zone

The area between the defensive rear court and settled net position. Players usually move through it rather than remain there for long.

## Common padel shots

### Bajada

An attacking shot played after a high rebound from the back glass, often struck while moving forward.

### Bandeja

A controlled sliced overhead used mainly to maintain net position after an opponent lobs.

### Block

A compact defensive volley that uses the opponent's pace rather than adding a large swing.

### Chiquita

A soft low ball toward the net players' feet, intended to force an upward volley and create space to advance.

### Drop shot

A soft shot designed to land short, usually when opponents are positioned deep enough for the empty space in front to matter.

### Forehand

A shot played on the racket side of the body.

### Backhand

A shot played across the opposite side of the body.

### Gancho

A hooked overhead played with a more compact, curved action than a full smash, often used when the ball is slightly behind or to the side of the ideal contact point.

### Kick smash

An overhead hit with topspin so the post-bounce trajectory climbs sharply, potentially carrying the ball out of the court.

### Lob

A high shot over the opponents intended to push them away from the net.

### Passing shot

A ball played past or between opponents at the net rather than directly at them.

### Reset lob

A safer, higher lob used mainly to escape pressure, recover time and rebuild the rally rather than attack immediately.

### Rulo

A topspin overhead, commonly directed toward the side fence or corner to create an awkward rebound.

### Smash

An attacking overhead played with greater pace to finish the point or create an unreturnable rebound.

### Víbora

An aggressive sliced overhead, generally faster and more attacking than a bandeja.

### X3

A smash that bounces in the opponents' court and leaves the enclosure over the three-metre side wall.

### X4

A smash that leaves the court over the four-metre back enclosure after bouncing in the opponents' court.

## Tactical terms

### Australian formation

A serving formation where the server's partner starts on the same side of the court, allowing pairs to preserve preferred left/right positions after the serve.

### Backhand side

In common doubles language, the right side of the court for a right-handed player, where many central balls arrive to the backhand.

### Defending pair

The pair positioned deeper in the court, typically trying to neutralise pressure and regain the net.

### Forehand side

In common doubles language, the left side for a right-handed player, giving that player's forehand and overhead more access to the middle.

### Middle ball

A ball travelling between partners. Pairs usually agree a priority rule to avoid hesitation or collisions.

### Net pair

The pair holding the attacking net position.

### Playing down the middle

Directing the ball between opponents. It reduces angles and can create uncertainty over who should take it.

### Transition

Movement between the defensive rear court and the attacking net position.

## Social and tournament formats

### Americano

A social rotation format where players commonly change partners and accumulate individual points across fixed-point rounds. Exact rules vary by organiser.

### Fixed Points

A scoring method where every rally counts as one point toward a target rather than using love, 15, 30 and 40.

### Mexicano

A social rotation format related to Americano where later pairings are commonly influenced by current ranking or performance.

### Round robin

A format where players or pairs meet multiple opponents within a group rather than being eliminated after one loss.

### Knockout

A tournament structure where losing a match removes a player or pair from the main draw.

## Equipment terms

### Balance

How the racket's weight is distributed between handle and head. Higher balance can feel more powerful but less manoeuvrable.

### Continental grip

A neutral racket grip commonly used for volleys, serves and overheads because it allows the racket face to adapt without large grip changes.

### Overgrip

A replaceable wrap placed over the racket handle for comfort, grip size and sweat absorption.

### Sweet spot

The area of the racket face that produces the cleanest, most forgiving contact.

### Wrist strap

The safety cord attached to a padel racket and worn around the wrist during play.

## Useful distinctions

**Tie-break ≠ super tie-break.** A normal tie-break is typically to seven; a super tie-break is commonly to ten.

**Golden Point ≠ Star Point.** They are different deuce systems.

**Fixed Points ≠ Americano.** Fixed Points describes how rallies are counted; Americano also describes player rotation and standings.

**Bandeja ≠ víbora.** Both are overheads, but a bandeja is generally more controlled while a víbora is more aggressive.

**Chiquita ≠ drop shot.** A chiquita is normally played low towards net players' feet; a drop shot tries to exploit empty short court when opponents are deeper.
