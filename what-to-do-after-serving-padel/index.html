Tactics & positioning · Updated 29 September 2026

The serve is only the first half of the pattern.

Once the ball leaves your racket, your job changes immediately: move forward, get balanced before the return arrives and play the first volley with enough control to establish the net with your partner.

Move as soon as you serve

Do not stand behind the service line watching whether the ball lands in. Start moving forward as the serve travels so you use the time you created. The aim is to arrive in a position where you can volley rather than receive the return around your feet.

The better your serve placement and depth, the more predictable that first movement becomes. If you are still working on the serve itself, read how to improve your padel serve.

Do not sprint blindly to the net

Moving forward does not mean charging until you are almost touching the net. You need enough time and space to react to the return, especially a fast ball towards your body or a lob over your head.

Think of the transition as controlled acceleration followed by a small balance step before the opponent contacts the return.

Split as the receiver hits

You want your feet available when the returner strikes the ball. If you are still running through contact, changing direction becomes difficult. A small split or stabilising step lets you react left, right, forwards or backwards.

The exact timing is more important than the exact distance from the net. Arriving perfectly on paper but still moving is worse than stopping slightly deeper in a balanced position.

Expect the first volley to be uncomfortable

The receiver is trying to stop you taking control of the net. A good return may arrive low, at your body or through the middle. Your first volley does not need to finish the point.

Use a compact action and prioritise a ball that keeps the opponents behind you. Deep middle is often a sensible target because it reduces angles and makes it harder for the receiving pair to immediately counterattack.

Move with your partner

Your partner is already at or moving around the net position while you serve. As you come forward, the pair should form one shape rather than leaving a large channel between you.

If the return is played wide, both players adjust towards that side. If the return is a good lob, both players need to recognise whether the pair can keep the net or should retreat together.

Let the serve influence your first position

A serve towards the side glass tends to encourage a different return pattern from a serve into the body or middle. You do not need to predict the exact shot, but your own target should tell you which angles are more likely.

This is the tactical value of a controlled serve: it does not just make the return harder, it reduces the number of comfortable options the receiver has.

Do not attack every first volley

Many players arrive at the net and immediately try to hit hard because they feel they should take advantage of the attacking position. That can waste a perfectly good service pattern.

A solid first volley at roughly controlled match pace is often enough. Make the opponents hit another ball from the back, then increase pressure when you receive something easier.

What if the return is a lob?

Read the quality of the lob rather than automatically forcing an overhead. A comfortable lob may allow a bandeja or víbora while you retain the net. A deeper lob that gets behind you should be respected: turn early and retreat rather than hitting while falling backwards.

Good service games are not about refusing to leave the net. They are about making good decisions from an attacking starting position.

The simple serve-plus-one pattern

For most recreational matches, remember this sequence:

Serve with a target → move forward immediately → balance as the receiver hits → play the first volley with margin → establish the net with your partner.

If that pattern becomes automatic, your service games feel calmer because you stop treating the serve and the first volley as unrelated shots.
