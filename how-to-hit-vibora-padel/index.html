Shots & technique · Updated 29 September 2026

The víbora is an attacking overhead, not a smash with a different name.

Use it when a lob gives you enough balance and height to attack with slice while still protecting the net. The shot works because of placement, spin and a low rebound, not because you swing as hard as possible.

Know when the víbora is the right choice

A víbora usually makes sense when you are still relatively close to the net and the lob is comfortable enough that you do not need the safer control of a bandeja. If you are moving hard backwards, reaching behind your head or losing balance, the more aggressive shot is often the wrong one.

The simplest distinction is purpose: a bandeja protects position, while a víbora asks the defenders a more difficult question.

Turn early and get side-on

As soon as you read the lob, rotate your shoulders and organise your feet. Good preparation gives you room to contact the ball in front and to the side of your body rather than directly above your head.

Do not wait underneath the ball and then try to create the shot with your arm. Most inconsistent víboras start with late movement rather than poor racket technique.

Prepare high but keep the swing compact

The racket starts high, with a continental-style grip commonly used for overheads. You need enough preparation to accelerate through the ball, but an enormous backswing makes timing harder and encourages you to chase power.

Think of the action as fast and controlled rather than long and violent.

Create slice through the ball

The defining quality of the víbora is the sliced, sidespin-heavy contact that creates an awkward rebound after the bounce. The racket travels through and across the ball rather than simply down on top of it.

The exact spin changes with the height and position of the ball. You do not need maximum spin. A clean strike with good direction is more valuable than brushing so much that the ball loses pace or floats short.

Contact in front and use your body

If you contact the ball behind your head, control disappears quickly. Move early enough that the ball is in front of your hitting shoulder and use the rotation of your body rather than trying to manufacture all the speed with the forearm and wrist.

A balanced contact also makes the next part easier: recovering towards the net instead of falling backwards after the shot.

Choose a target that makes the rebound difficult

Deep corners and the space towards the side wall can make the sliced rebound uncomfortable, but there is no value in aiming for centimetres of margin every time. A firm víbora into a sensible deep target often creates more pressure than a spectacular attempt that clips the fence or glass first.

Against defenders who struggle with the walls, depth and spin can be enough. Against stronger players, changing direction and pace becomes more important.

Recover after the shot

The víbora is normally part of keeping an attacking position. Once you hit, move forwards again with your partner and prepare for the next ball. Standing where you struck the overhead leaves space in front of you and gives away much of the advantage you created.

Common víbora mistakes

Using it from too far back: the shot becomes a desperate overhead instead of controlled pressure.

Trying to hit a winner: overswinging reduces spin, margin and recovery.

Late preparation: you end up underneath the ball and contact behind your body.

Too much wrist: timing becomes unstable. Build the shot around body position and a repeatable swing first.

Forgetting the next ball: a good víbora is often a setup shot, not the end of the rally.

Choose the overhead before you swing.
