# Americano vs Mexicano: which padel format should you play?

Both formats are popular ways to play lots of short doubles matches with different partners. The main difference is how the next set of teams is chosen, and that changes the whole feel of the evening.

## The quick distinction

**Americano** usually follows a planned rotation so players meet different partners and opponents across the session. **Mexicano** uses the results from completed rounds to influence later pairings, often bringing players with similar standings together. In either format, the event is typically played as a series of short fixed-point matches rather than conventional sets.

Neither label gives you a completely universal rulebook. Clubs can disagree about points per round, tie-breaks, rest compensation and what happens when players join late. Always tell participants which variation you intend to use, particularly if the results will produce a final ranking.

## How the team rotation works

An Americano draw can be prepared before the session, subject to the available players, courts and planned number of rounds. The organiser can aim to distribute partners, opponents and off-court turns fairly. When the goal is meeting more people, that predictability is a strength. Players know the format is designed around variety rather than continually moving them up or down by form.

For Mexicano, the organiser confirms the latest scores before deciding on the next round. The standings provide the basis for suggested groupings. That can produce more closely matched games as the session goes on, but it also makes the event sensitive to incomplete or incorrect score entry. Published match assignments should not silently change if an older result is corrected.

## How are points recorded?

Many social groups agree a fixed combined rally target for each match, such as 16, 24 or 32. Consider a 16-point game: if one pair wins 10 rallies and the other wins 6, the result is **10–6** . Each player on the first team typically earns 10 individual points towards the event leaderboard, while each opponent earns 6. A result of 8–8 is possible and represents a drawn match in formats that permit ties.

This is separate from traditional 15, 30 and 40 scoring. The target should be agreed before starting and should remain clear when the organiser or players submit results. For a deeper explanation, read the [Fixed Points scoring guide](https://padelwrist.com/fixed-points-padel-scoring/) .

## Which should you choose for your group?

Choose Americano if the point of the evening is to get new players mixing, make the schedule relatively predictable and keep the event friendly to a wide range of abilities. It's especially suitable when the social element matters as much as the final ranking.

Choose Mexicano if your participants enjoy competitive short games and you want later rounds to reflect how players are performing. This can keep strong players challenged and give newer players opponents who are closer to their level, depending on the pairing rules the organiser uses.

Neither format automatically solves uneven player counts. When you have more players than can fit on the courts, rest turns and ranking fairness need explicit treatment. A simple raw total may disadvantage somebody who has played fewer matches.

## What should the organiser agree beforehand?

Record the number of players and courts, the score target, whether you are aiming for a certain number of rounds or playing until time runs out, and how the winner will be determined. Set a rule for tied scores, equal overall points, byes, withdrawals, substitutions and corrections.

When running Mexicano, add a clear policy for when future pairings become official. The draw should be proposed, checked and published only after earlier results are accepted. If a previous score changes, the organiser should decide whether a later unpublished proposal needs reviewing.
