# The net is where you apply pressure. Getting there is only half the job.

Padel is not won by standing as close to the net as possible. The advantage comes from controlling a position where you can volley before the ball drops, reduce the opponents' time and make them defend from behind you.

## Why the net changes the rally

From the back, many of your shots must travel upwards to clear the net and then survive the opponents' volleys. From the net, you can contact the ball earlier and direct it downwards or low into difficult spaces.

That difference in contact height and time is why so much padel strategy revolves around taking, keeping or recovering the net.

## Do not run forward behind a bad ball

Moving to the net only helps if the shot before it creates enough time or pressure. A short lob can leave you running forwards while the opponents are preparing an easy overhead.

The classic transition is a deep lob that pushes the opposing pair backwards. Once you have forced them to retreat, you and your partner advance together.

## The serve is your built-in route forward

Unlike a defensive rally, the serve gives you a predictable moment to move. After serving, start advancing immediately, then balance as the receiver makes contact so you can react to the return.

The goal is not to sprint all the way to the net before the ball comes back. It is to arrive in a position where the first volley is playable and the pair can establish an attacking shape. See [what to do after serving](https://padelwrist.com/what-to-do-after-serving-padel/) for the full serve-plus-one pattern.

## How close should you stand?

There is no single measurement that fits every rally. Too close and you become vulnerable to lobs and balls at your body. Too far back and low volleys drop around your feet.

Think in terms of being close enough to volley comfortably while leaving enough reaction space to move backwards for a lob. Your position constantly adjusts to the quality of the opponent's contact.

## Move as a pair

If the ball goes wide to one side, both net players shift to protect the most dangerous angles. If one player is forced backwards by a lob, the pair usually needs to retreat together rather than leaving one player isolated at the net.

The net position is a team shape, not two independent spots.

## Keeping the net is why the bandeja exists

A deep lob tests whether you can defend your attacking position without giving it away. The bandeja is a controlled overhead used when a full smash would be risky. Play it deep, recover forwards and ask the opponents to solve another difficult ball.

When the lob is more comfortable and you can attack with greater balance, a [víbora](https://padelwrist.com/how-to-hit-vibora-padel/) can add pressure while still preserving the attacking position.

## When should you deliberately retreat?

If a lob beats you, retreat early rather than attempting a desperate overhead while falling backwards. Likewise, if your partner is driven back and the pair shape has broken, recovering together can be safer than trying to hold an attacking position alone.

Good net play includes knowing when the net is temporarily lost.

## The simplest tactical loop in padel

At beginner and intermediate level, a huge amount of padel can be understood as this loop:

**Defend from the back → create a good lob → move forward together → volley and use overheads to keep the net → retreat together if the lob wins.**

On serve, you enter that loop from the attacking side: serve, move forward, play the first volley with margin, then try to keep the net.
