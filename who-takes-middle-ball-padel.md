# The middle belongs to the player with the better ball, not automatically the player standing closer to it.

Middle balls create confusion because both partners can reach them. A useful priority system considers forehand or backhand, height, who is moving forward, the direction of the ball and what the pair has agreed before the point.

## There is no universal “left player takes middle” rule

That shortcut can be useful for a particular pair, but it is not a law of padel. Handedness alone can completely change which player has the stronger central contact.

## High balls often favour the stronger overhead

For two right-handed players, the left-side player's forehand and overhead may naturally cover more of the centre. With a left-hander on the right, both partners can have a forehand towards the middle, so communication becomes even more important.

## Low defensive balls may favour the player already behind them

If one player has to cross dramatically in front of the partner to reach a low ball, taking it may break the pair's shape. Sometimes the safer player is the one with the simpler path, even if the other has a nominally stronger stroke.

## Movement direction matters

A player moving forwards into the ball may have a much cleaner contact than a partner moving sideways or backwards. Do not apply a static ownership rule to a dynamic rally.

## Call early

If both players wait until the ball is between them, the decision has already become difficult. Use a clear “mine” or “yours” as soon as ownership is obvious, particularly on lobs and slower middle balls.

## After the shot, recover the shape

Taking a middle ball can pull one player across the court. The other player should shift to cover the space that has opened, and both should recover once the shot is played.

## Agree defaults, then allow exceptions

Before a match, decide who normally takes high centre balls, who calls lobs and how a left-hander changes the pattern. During the rally, the better-positioned player can override that default with a clear call.

## A simple way to practise ownership

Have a feeder send slow balls into the middle at different heights while both partners start in normal positions. Before either player swings, one of you must call the ball. After each feed, briefly check whether the chosen player had the cleaner path and whether the other partner moved to cover the space.

The aim is not to create one rigid rule. It is to make the common decisions familiar enough that a real match no longer ends with two rackets colliding or both players leaving the same ball.
